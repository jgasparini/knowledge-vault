## Summary

A short explainer on the pre-mortem technique: instead of asking a team what could go wrong, you ask them to imagine the project has already failed and work backward to explain why. Naming a specific failure up front, before the plan is locked in, gives people cover to raise doubts they'd otherwise sit on through a normal risk-assessment meeting.

## Key insights

- Naming a specific failure scenario up front gives people permission to voice doubts they'd normally keep to themselves in a standard risk-assessment meeting.
- The exercise works best early, before a plan is finalized — running it after the plan is locked in blunts the effect.
- Each person writes down their own reasons for the imagined failure independently before anyone speaks out loud. That independent-writing step is what stops the first answer from anchoring everyone else's guesses.
- Teams report catching risks tied to unstated assumptions and overly optimistic scheduling — the kind of thing standard status reviews tend to miss.
- It's framed as a one-time exercise repeated at major project milestones, not a substitute for a live, ongoing risk register.
